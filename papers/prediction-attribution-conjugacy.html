Prediction–Attribution Conjugacy in Adaptive Decision Problems

Honggao Cao · October 2026

When can outcomes help us distinguish competing explanations and decide what to do?

What this paper helps us understand

A reliable forecast can still leave a decision maker unsure how to act. Different explanations may fit familiar observations yet imply different consequences under a new condition. This paper asks when such comparisons can help us learn which relationships matter for a decision, and why an intervention that works may weaken the signal used to predict the original problem. It separates that diagnostic task from learning over time and from changes caused by action.

Abstract

Many consequential decisions require both a forecast and an account of which parts or relations within a system matter for action. We examine adaptive decision problems in which decision makers consider structural accounts, make prospective predictions, act, and revise their understanding as outcomes become available. Prediction and attribution become diagnostically reciprocal when competing accounts imply different outcomes under the same informative condition, allowing later observations to change their relative credibility. We call this relationship prediction–attribution conjugacy. The framework distinguishes that diagnostic core from learning and from the system’s response to action. These distinctions help explain why routine predictive stability may leave attribution unresolved, why successful intervention can weaken an old predictive signal, and why diagnostic learning may be overtaken by structural change. Examples from finance, clinical trials, and public policy show how the usefulness of the framework depends on the decision, the available diagnostic comparisons, and what action changes.
