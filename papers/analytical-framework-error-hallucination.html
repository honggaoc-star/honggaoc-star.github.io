An Analytical Framework for Error and Hallucination in Deployed Generative AI Systems

Honggao Cao · September 2026

How should a delivered AI response be evaluated before its cause is investigated?

What this paper helps us understand

An AI response may agree with the material supplied to a system yet conflict with the source or instruction that should govern the task. And finding an error does not reveal what caused it. This paper organizes evaluation around those two distinctions: establish the governing standard before classifying a response, then use separate production evidence to investigate its cause.

Abstract

Judging a response from a deployed generative AI system requires an applicable standard; explaining its production requires a different evidentiary basis. These requirements can diverge: information used in production may differ from the information that should govern evaluation, and a valid error classification may leave the production cause unresolved. We develop an authority-first framework that addresses these problems in sequence. The evaluator establishes the task and applicable authority policy, identifies assessable assertions and task obligations, and selects the governing standards. Assertions are classified as supported, contradicted, unsupported, or unresolved; obligations are assessed as satisfied, violated, or unresolved. These parallel comparisons support two related error classifications. Hallucination concerns a representational assertion contradicted by, or lacking support required from, its governing reference. Task-obligation fidelity error concerns failure to satisfy an applicable requirement. The classifications can overlap when a response both violates a requirement and misrepresents compliance or the requirement itself. We then distinguish identifying an error from locating its possible production source and attributing its cause. Applications to temporal change, continuing-project state, propagation, and retrieval-augmented generation show how the distinction guides evaluation of configured systems. Consequences inform the urgency of a response, while supported production findings inform the choice of controls. The framework supplies a conceptual sequence whose effects on evaluator agreement and control selection remain questions for empirical study.
