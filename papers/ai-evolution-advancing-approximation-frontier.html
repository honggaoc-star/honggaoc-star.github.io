AI Evolution as an Advancing Approximation Frontier

Honggao Cao · October 2026

How might successful AI progress change the development frontier itself?

What this paper helps us understand

An improvement in AI capability can change what developers need to investigate next. A remaining limitation may reflect an unresolved task, a gap in evaluation, or changes in the practices against which a system is judged. This paper helps readers distinguish these possibilities and ask what evidence could separate competing explanations while development continues. Its account leaves open both the existence of a fixed capability ceiling and which architectures might come next.

Abstract

AI development is often described as progress toward increasingly capable systems. This paper asks a different question: how does successful progress change the development problems that remain? It treats AI development as an advancing approximation process in which artificial systems increasingly reproduce, exceed, or substitute for functions associated with intelligent human performance through mechanisms that can differ substantially from those that generate human intelligence.

We develop a conditional account of frontier change. Differential resolution is one mechanism: when some development problems resolve faster than others, progress can change the composition of the unresolved frontier rather than simply reduce its size. This motivates the empirical hypothesis that dimensions with weakly specified or difficult-to-evaluate targets may resolve more slowly than dimensions with clearer targets and feedback. Other mechanisms can change the frontier through different routes: capability can outrun evaluation, human practices and evaluative standards can alter the reference conditions for later systems, and AI can increasingly participate in the development of successor systems.

These mechanisms also create a diagnostic problem. Developers may face several plausible explanations of a current limitation, each implying different interventions, while the system being diagnosed continues to change. We use Prediction–Attribution Conjugacy, developed in earlier work, to examine when such explanations are structurally distinguishable, when they are identifiable in practice, and when diagnostic learning may race against frontier movement.

The central contribution of the paper is an account of how AI progress can change the composition, visibility, reference conditions, and developmental structure of the problems that come next. Questions about a fixed AI ceiling or a particular architectural succession remain open.
